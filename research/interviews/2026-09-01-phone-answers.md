## What the cold answers did to the documents

### Q2 — he named the mission, unprompted

Asked only *"what was that camp there for?"*, with no phrase offered and no alternatives to
choose from, he answered: **"The best place to have readios because it was high."** He then
explained the physics himself — flat flooded country for a hundred miles, sets that "could
transmit 25 miles," a correspondent forty miles off, and the mountain lifting the signal over
"the curvature of the Earth."

The order of battle read the same day prints the camp's official mission as **"MISSION: RADIO
RELAY"** (`data/sources/radix-sf-order-of-battle`). His testimony and the official
designation match, and he supplied his reasoning before anyone showed him the document.
**This is the strongest corroboration of Larry's account the archive has produced.**

### Q4 — the boxing scheme has siblings, and they are the predicted ones

He gave **"Body Jab 42, Right Cross, Upper Cut,"** and thought there **"Might have been a
Body Jab 41, 43,"** with **Major Schwartzkoff** over them.

On 2026-08-23 this archive wrote that his boxing-terms rule "predicts its siblings — an
element using 'Body Jab' would plausibly have used other boxing terms (hook, uppercut,
southpaw, counter, clinch) for related stations" (`../units/callsigns-and-units.md`).
**Uppercut was on that predicted list.** He has now produced it cold, along with Right Cross,
and a numbering series 41/42/43 that supports the standing hypothesis that the digits mark
the station within a net rather than being part of the call word.

Note the format difference against the period source found today: the UTT/68th memoir records
the Đồng Xoài camp radio as **"55 Body Jab"** — number first, and a different number. Larry
says **"Body Jab 42"** — number last. Both orders are recorded; neither is corrected.

### Q1 — a name, and it is not the name in the roster

Asked who was in charge, he said **"Special Forces ran the camp. Captain Kennedy was in
Charge of the camp most of the time. But I really worked for 1st Cavalry."**

The order of battle lists **a 1LT Lewis commanding at Núi Bà Đen from 20 April 1965**
(`../people/nui-ba-den-roster-names.md`). He did not say Lewis. That is a clean cold answer
and it is left standing as a difference, not reconciled: a detachment commander of record and
the man Larry experienced as running the place need not be the same person, and TDY rotations
through this site were constant.

What it does do is **upgrade Captain Kennedy** (`data/people/captain-kennedy.json`). Kennedy
was already at the mountain in this archive — tentatively identified in VN-0006, and firmly
credited with taking VN-0023 on patrol. He is now, in Larry's words, the man in charge of the
camp most of the time. The person record should move accordingly once fidelity is settled.

**"But I really worked for 1st Cavalry"** goes to ledger #2 and issue #10. It is his most
direct statement yet of the 1st Cavalry association, and it still sits against the fact that
the 1st Cavalry Division did not arrive in country until about September 1965. Recorded, not
resolved.

### Q3 — the camp's own name

**"They called the camp The Mountain. The guys in my Unit in Saigon called it the mountain."**
A plain-language answer that also confirms the Saigon end of the net was his own unit.

## The new material, which nobody asked for

### Đồng Xoài, and a man named Gagne

Answering the question about *why the camp existed*, he volunteered an account of being at the
radio during an attack on a Special Forces camp — and named the camp: **Đồng Xoài**. Roughly
sixteen defenders, "about 4000 enemy all at once at 4am," the man on the other radio "dying
from shot in the stomach," and **"Gagne was with me and died, he was a french foreign
Legionair."**

**Gagne appears nowhere in this archive.** It is a new name, and a death.

Independently, and before this call, the day's research had found the Đồng Xoài battle of
**9–10 June 1965** in a UTT/68th Aviation Company memoir, which records the Special Forces
camp's radio call sign as "55 Body Jab" and its radio operator as a lieutenant later
identified as a Medal of Honor recipient (`../units/callsigns-and-units.md`). Larry was not
told any of this.

**Tensions to hold open, not to resolve:**

- The memoir has the camp's radio officer **rescued alive**; Larry remembers the man on the
  radio **dying of a stomach wound**. Both may be true of different men on different radios,
  or his memory may carry what he believed at the time from what he could hear. Do not
  reconcile these by preferring one.
- The published battle began in the **night of 9 June**; he says **4 a.m.**
- **Where was he?** The answer runs together the mountain and the radio without saying which
  set he was sitting at. This matters and is asked below.

### The CBC film crew

He says there are **photographs of the CBC crew among his own pictures**, that they filmed for
about two weeks, that he was asked questions on camera, and that they all ate together —
possibly around Christmas 1964. Canadian Broadcasting Corporation documentaries of that period
are catalogued and in some cases held by the CBC archives, so this is a genuinely traceable
thread, and one that could end in footage of Larry at twenty-one.

### On Gagne

> "He mentioned on the call the guy was french. He also mentioned that Legionaires couldn't
> go home."

**This narrows the Gagne question without closing it, and the distinction matters enough to
write down.** The French Foreign Legion recruits foreigners by design — a Legionnaire could
be German, Spanish, Algerian or American and still be a Legionnaire. So *"the guy was french"*
may be reporting **the unit rather than the passport**, and nationality is still the open
variable that decides which casualty records could ever name him.

**#68 therefore stays closed to searching.** The rule has not changed: run a search against
the wrong universe of records, find nothing, and the false negative can stand for years. The
family has offered to ask him directly, and the interview guide and `/interview/` now carry
the sharpened question: *"You mentioned Gagne, and that he was Foreign Legion. Was he French
himself, or was he from somewhere else? And where was he when he died?"*

The second half is the more interesting half. **"Legionnaires couldn't go home"** matches a
real and documented feature of the Legion — enlistment under an assumed identity, and men
with reasons not to return to their own countries. It is not a detail a person invents by
accident. It is a lead about **who Gagne was**, not a fact about him.

### On the python — VN-0153

> "My father said they did and it tasted delicious."

Answers the standing question on VN-0153 (*"Did they eat it?"*). Small, and worth having: it
is the first thing anyone has established about that photograph, which is otherwise

**The follow-up now in the guide is the one that could date it:** *"Who caught it, and where
were you?"* Nothing else will.
