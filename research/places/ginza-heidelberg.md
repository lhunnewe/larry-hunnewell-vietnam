# The "Heidelberg" — the downstairs German restaurant off the Ginza (fall 1965)

**Research note, 2026-08-12.** Larry remembers (call of 2026-08-12) that during his hospital
stay in Japan he and the friend from the next bed went to "the Ginza strip," found a sign
that said "Heidelberg" for a German restaurant that was downstairs, and ate there. Web
research into whether such a restaurant existed.

## Status: the name is unverified; the scene is completely credible

A restaurant literally named **"Heidelberg" (ハイデルベルク) on or near the Ginza in the
mid-1960s could not be confirmed** from any reachable open-web source, English or Japanese.
That is *not* proof it didn't exist — online coverage of individual 1960s Ginza storefronts
is thin, and the decisive archives are paywalled or blocked to automated search (see next
steps). One period source says **about seven establishments served German food in
early-1960s Tokyo**, of which only four are named in accessible sources — a "Heidelberg"
among the unnamed three is entirely plausible. His memory stands as given.

## The strongest documented match: Ketel's (ケテル)

Every detail of the memory *except the name* matches **Ketel's Restaurant**, Ginza 5-5-14
(Hideyoshi Building, Namiki-dōri — squarely "the Ginza strip"):

- It occupied the **1st floor and the basement**, and the **basement was the main dining
  room** — you went downstairs to eat, exactly as he described.
- Japan's oldest German restaurant: opened 1930 by Helmut Ketel, a WWI German POW who stayed,
  next to his Bar Rheingold; famous **beer-barrel-shaped entrance**; Ian Fleming was taken
  there in 1962. It was *the* place a foreigner in 1965 Tokyo was steered to for German food.
  Operated until 2004; building demolished ~2006.
- The mismatch is real: its signage would have read "Ketel's"/"Rheingold," not "Heidelberg."
  ("Alt Heidelberg" was, however, a common German-romantic motif on beer-hall posters and
  menus — the name he read may have been on such a board.)

Other German-food landmarks of the era, for completeness: **Lohmeyer** (ローマイヤ, Ginza
Namiki-dōri, since 1925 — not basement-level per sources); **Ginza Lion** beer hall (7-chōme,
Japanese-run, ground floor — famous among GIs from its 1945–52 occupation-forces years);
basement beer halls Pilsen and Munchen (Japanese-run, not named Heidelberg).

## Next steps (in order of promise)

1. **Pacific Stars and Stripes, Sept–Nov 1965** — its restaurant ads targeted exactly this
   reader. Full run searchable on newspapers.com (Publishers Extra, paywalled). Search
   "Heidelberg," "German restaurant Ginza," "Ketel," "rathskeller."
2. **NDL Next Digital Library** (lab.ndl.go.jp/dl) — free full-text search of digitized
   Shōwa-era books/magazines; search 「ハイデルベルク 銀座」 in a real browser (JS-only site;
   automated fetches can't query it).
3. **1965 Tokyo classified telephone directory** (職業別電話帳) at the National Diet
   Library — a definitive yes/no on a listed ハイデルベルク in Chūō-ku.
4. **Archive.org "search inside"** (free account) on *This Is Japan 1965*
   (`thisisjapan19650000na`), *Fodor's Japan and East Asia 1966* (`fodorsguidetojap0000euge`),
   the JNTO *New Official Guide* 1964/1966, and *Tokyo: VIP's Confidential Guide* — search
   "Heidelberg."
5. **Interview probe (cheap, possibly decisive):** ask Larry what he remembers about the
   place — the sign, the entrance, the stairs — *without supplying details*. If he
   spontaneously describes a giant **beer-barrel doorway**, that's Ketel's signature. Also:
   was the sign in roman letters? Could "Heidelberg" have been on a poster or menu board
   rather than over the door?
