# Which hospital was Larry in, in Japan? (Sept–Nov 1965)

**Research note, 2026-08-12.** Web research into the U.S. Army medical system in Japan in fall
1965, prompted by Larry's account (call of 2026-08-12) that he was medevaced from Vietnam
~early September 1965 with hepatitis-like illness, malaria, and infections, and by his
DD-214's last duty assignment: "…USA MED COMD, APO SF 96343, USARJ."

## Answer: almost certainly the Camp Zama hospital (~90% confidence)

**The U.S. Army Medical Center, Japan** — the Army hospital at **Sagami-Ōno, Sagamihara**,
adjacent to Camp Zama (redesignated *U.S. Army Hospital, Camp Zama* on January 19, 1966) —
is the only realistic candidate. Four lines of evidence converge:

1. **It was effectively the only Army hospital in Japan receiving Vietnam evacuees in
   September–November 1965.** The official AMEDD histories date every alternative: the 7th
   Field Hospital arrived from Fort Knox in **November 1965** (Johnson AFB, Iruma); the
   **249th General Hospital** became operational at Camp Drake in **December 1965**; the
   **106th General Hospital** at Kishine Barracks (Yokohama) also became operational in
   **December 1965**. A soldier evacuated in early September 1965 could not have been
   admitted to any of those three — and by December, Larry was already home (separated
   November 30, 1965).
2. **Disease evacuees went there in exactly his window.** Per the AMEDD internal-medicine
   history (citing Reed, Feinstein & Steiger, JAMA 1968), **462 falciparum-malaria patients
   were admitted to the U.S. Army Hospital, Camp Zama between August and December 1965.**
   The hospital was the receiving point for Vietnam malaria — and by extension hepatitis —
   evacuees in precisely September–November 1965. It was a small (~100-bed) hospital in 1965,
   rapidly expanding under the Vietnam load (1,000 beds authorized March 1966).
3. **The DD-214 APO matches.** APO San Francisco **96343 is Camp Zama's APO**, used by USARJ
   headquarters and its tenant medical units.
4. **The Ginza day trip fits.** Sagami-Ōno is ~40 km southwest of Tokyo, on the Odakyū line —
   about 40 minutes to Shinjuku, then a short hop to the Ginza. A routine day out for an
   ambulatory convalescent. (Camp Drake and Kishine weren't open anyway.)

**Established vs. inferred:** the operational dates, the Aug–Dec 1965 Camp Zama malaria
cohort, and APO 96343 = Camp Zama are *established* from official histories. That Larry
personally was at Camp Zama is *inferred* — but it is the only candidate consistent with his
dates and his DD-214 APO. The clincher would be his clinical file (below).

## Reading the DD-214 line

"…USA MED COMD, APO SF 96343, USARJ" = **U.S. Army Medical Command, Japan**, the USARJ
medical headquarters at Camp Zama that ran the hospital (later formally "HQ US Army Medical
Command, Japan (627th Hospital Center), South Camp Zama" — the 627th arrived in 1966).
Patients evacuated from Vietnam were dropped from their combat unit and attached to the
receiving facility's **medical holding detachment**, carried on the medical command's rolls
until return to duty or separation. A last-duty line naming the medical command with the Camp
Zama APO is exactly what a soldier separated *from patient status* would show — and it
explains why the DD-214 never names his Vietnam unit. ~~The illegible prefix before "USA MED
COMD" is plausibly "PAT DET," "MED HOLD DET," or "HQ" — worth re-checking the photo of the
form with these readings in mind.~~ **Confirmed 2026-08-15:** a clear scan of the DD-214
reads the prefix as "**MHD**" — Medical Holding Detachment. Larry separated from patient
status; the inference above is now documentary
(`research/records-request/dd214-findings.md`).

## The 1965 evacuation pipeline (for the timeline narrative)

In-country holding policy in 1965 was only ~15 days, so serious hepatitis/malaria cases were
flown out quickly: scheduled medevac flights from Tan Son Nhut / Cam Ranh / Da Nang by C-118
and C-130 to **Clark AB, Philippines**, then onward to Japan — in the early days on C-54s and
C-124s into **Tachikawa AB**, through the Air Force casualty staging facility there, then by
ground ambulance to the Army hospital. The flood of malaria/hepatitis evacuees leaving
Vietnam is exactly why the 6th Convalescent Center was stood up at Cam Ranh Bay (opened
November 29, 1965) — Larry left just before the Army began holding such patients in-country.

## How to get his hospital records (the actionable step)

**Clinical (inpatient) records are NOT in the personnel file.** Since 1960 they have been
retired to NPRC **filed by the name of the treating hospital**, not by soldier — which also
means the 1973 NPRC fire (Army personnel files 1912–1960) does not implicate them.

On the SF-180 (or via eVetRecs/milConnect if Larry or next-of-kin files):
1. Check medical/clinical records and state explicitly: *"Clinical (inpatient) records of
   treatment at **U.S. Army Hospital, Camp Zama (U.S. Army Medical Center, Japan)**,
   Sagami-Ōno, Japan, **September–November 1965**, for hepatitis and malaria"* — plus name,
   service number, DOB. NPRC needs the hospital name, month/year, and identifiers; expect
   inpatient narrative summaries, discharge summaries, etc.
2. Belt-and-suspenders: also list the alternate facility names ("U.S. Army Medical Center,
   Japan" — the official 1965 designation — and "USAH Camp Zama"), and ask NPRC to check the
   **7th Field Hospital (Johnson AB, Nov 1965)** if the Zama search is negative. NA Form
   13042 is the locator form for medical records.
3. Also request the full OMPF and the separation physical, which may summarize the
   hospitalization even if the clinical file is thin.
