## `data/places/japan.json`

**summary** (before):

> Larry finished his service in Japan as a medical-evacuation patient. By his account (August 2026 call), he was flown out of Vietnam seriously ill — likely hepatitis, plus malaria and infections — arriving, as he remembers it, in early September 1965, with the weather turning cold into winter. His DD-214 independently confirms the Japan chapter: his last duty assignment was a U.S. Army medical command at APO SF 96343 (Camp Zama) under U.S. Army Japan. From the hospital he befriended the patient in the bed across from him, a man from back east whose name he no longer remembers; together they explored Tokyo, finding a basement German restaurant called "Heidelberg" off the Ginza. Japan is also where he remembers seeing the Stars and Stripes item reporting him killed in action. In a Tell a Story comment (2026-08-13) he named the hospital himself: "I was at Camp Zama in Japan in Sugami Uro" [read as Sagami-Ōno].

**researchNotes** (before):

> Larry has now named the hospital unprompted (2026-08-13 comment): told only that research had a high-confidence candidate — not which one — he wrote "Camp Zama ... in Sugami Uro" [Sagami-Ōno], converging exactly with the documentary case below. The hospital was almost certainly the U.S. Army Medical Center, Japan at Sagami-Ōno / Camp Zama — in Sept–Nov 1965 effectively the only Army hospital in Japan receiving Vietnam evacuees (the 249th General at Camp Drake and the 106th General at Kishine both opened December 1965, after he was home), and documented as admitting 462 Vietnam malaria patients in Aug–Dec 1965; APO 96343 is Camp Zama's. Full research note: research/places/japan-hospital-1965.md. Sagami-Ōno is ~40 minutes from Tokyo on the Odakyū line, an easy day trip to the Ginza for a convalescent. Remaining goals: confirm via NPRC clinical records (filed by hospital name — see research/records-request/); locate the original Stars and Stripes item and ask whether he saw it in the hospital ward; identify the German restaurant of the Ginza day (working leads and next archives in research/places/ginza-heidelberg.md); identify the hospital friend (a fellow patient from back east). Captain "Guegler" may be associated with this period. Coordinates wait on Larry confirming the hospital. 2026-08-23 (giscus-stories-c18118868): Larry puts the hospital stay at "close to 4 months" and describes the route — "Flew first to the Philippines and spent 4 days at a navy base or navy hospital. Then flown to camp Zama in Japan." The Philippines stop is new and is a documentary node in its own right (Clark AB or a Navy facility, ~4 days, early September 1965), worth naming on the NPRC clinical-records request alongside Camp Zama. Four months from an early-September arrival runs to about the end of December — later than the 30 November 1965 separation on the DD-214, so the duration is a soft estimate.

## `data/places/nui-ba-den.json`

**alternateNames** (before):

- Black Virgin Mountain
- "Sun Mountain" (as Larry may have called it)

**summary** (before):

> An isolated mountain rising from the plain near Tây Ninh in southern Vietnam, site of significant U.S. military activity including a signal and relay presence on its summit. Larry's association became much more specific in his 2026-08-13 comments: he stood search-and-rescue duty at the Special Forces camp there — call sign Body Jab 42 — near the old French fort, photographed a rocket launcher being mounted on the summit (VN-0006), remembers Sgt. Lowry stationed on top, and places the "450 civilians" accusation here at the very end of his tour. On 2026-09-05 he filled in the camp's daily life from the photographs: sick call for the Cambodian strikers and their families, run by Larry and a New Zealand medic he called Kiwi while Captain Kennedy "handled the political problems"; the residents' shelters built of scrap, "to get a stick like that you would go down into the jungle and carry it up"; the machine-gun nest on the rocks above; the mortar pit he ran for at night when the camp was attacked, and the bamboo ladder he ran into in the dark that knocked him cold for hours; cutting trees with explosive cord by the helicopter pad; a Cambodian chief who came in wearing a necklace of thirty human ears; and the quiet — "I enjoyed being on Nui Ba Den because of it being quiet. I could read and I stayed up at night with the Special Forces guys in case we were attacked." He read James Bond, le Carré, Louis L'Amour and Zane Grey there.

**researchNotes** (before):

> The mountain's location is settled geography; what remains unverified is the nature and dates of Larry's association with it. His 2026-08-13 testimony adds corroborating texture — the French fort matches the mountain's documented colonial ruins, and the two green berets visible in VN-0006 match the Special Forces presence he describes — but the photo's location is itself his identification, so the classification stays as recollection pending documentary support. The "Sun Mountain" name should be checked against period American nicknames. Special Forces and signal activity on the summit during 1964-1965 should be researched, including any Sergeant Lowry/Lowery and the camp call sign Body Jab 42 (see research/events/450-civilians-nui-ba-den.md). Research sweep 2026-08-13: the official Army history (Kelly, U.S. Army Special Forces 1961–1971) documents a CIDG camp at Núi Bà Đen itself in October 1964 manned by a half-size SF element — 1 officer, 5 enlisted, ~175 CIDG strikers — matching Larry's memory of a skeleton crew; the summit was taken by SF helicopter assault in May 1964, Detachment B-32 ran Tây Ninh province from January 1965, and the well-known 121st Signal Battalion relay ('Granite Romeo Tango') only arrived February 1966, so Larry's radio-relay window falls in the sparsely documented SF-relay period. 'French Fort on the back side of Nui Ba Den' is attested verbatim as an American place name. On this documentary fit the association is upgraded to plausible; direct confirmation of Larry's presence awaits his NPRC records. Archive session 2026-09-01 (research/places/nui-ba-den-locations.md): a veteran-compiled index of American positions, built from unit reports (sources/kindred-fsb-lz-index), gives the Special Forces camp here a designation and a date for the first time — Detachment A-324 (with A-325A also listed), opened July 1964, at grid XT281582 on 1:50,000 map sheet 6231-4 — and independently describes the summit as a Special Forces radio relay in that period, which is the part of the record that had been thinnest. Future records requests can now name Det A-324 rather than describe the camp. The same index also shows a cluster of positions on that sheet all called “French Fort,” around XT274686–XT289689 some eleven kilometres north of the summit — one prominent colonial work known to every American unit that passed through, occupied and renamed repeatedly between 1966 and 1971. It is recorded as a candidate for the fort Larry describes and not as an identification: the index is built from U.S. unit reports of 1966 and later, so a smaller ruin at the mountain’s base used by a half A-team in 1964–65 could be absent from it altogether. The 1:25,000 Pictomap sheets for 6231-4, which the archive supplies on request, should show fortification symbols directly and settle it. A photograph in a 5th Special Forces Group veteran's collection at the Library of Congress (sources/loc-vhp-chivers-nui-ba-den-1965) is captioned “Aerial view of Nui Ba Den (Black Lady) Mountain A-324 during air-drop, December 25, 1965” — the earliest independent use found of the designation by a man who served there rather than by a compiler, and, read with the rest of that collection, evidence that the summit was resupplied by air-drop. 2026-09-12, at the photographs: "The mountain is 2970 feet with the cloud general lower than the Mountain or right on us" (VN-0036); "It takes 27 hours to walk up the mountain" (VN-0037); a .30-calibre machine gun "on top of the rock where the sandbags with the ladder on the right to enter it" (VN-0035); and a patrol "to the left of Nui Ba Den" on which Sgt Lowry found the prisoner in VN-0023. His height is his figure. The coordinates here fix the summit, but no source cited on this record gives its elevation; that check is not made here and the figure is not corrected.

**alternateNames** (before):

- Ton Suhn Nhut / Tah Suhn Nhut (his son's transcriptions, 2026-09-03 and 2026-09-05)

**summary** (before):

> The location Larry remembers as his Company Headquarters — the place he radioed from Núi Bà Đen on the day of the "450 civilians" accusation, and where a medic diagnosed his jaundice days before the medevac. In his own written comments (2026-08-13) Larry spells it "Ton Son Nuht"; the earlier spelling "Thom Son Nuht" was his son's transcription of the name as Larry says it aloud. The leading research hypothesis is Tân Sơn Nhứt (Tan Son Nhut), the major air base adjacent to Saigon; Larry's own spelling is an even closer phonetic match, but the identification still awaits unit records.

**researchNotes** (before):

> Provenance upgraded 2026-08-13: the name is now attested in Larry's own writing ("Ton Son Nuht", twice, in his giscus comments), replacing the earlier secondhand transcription "Thom Son Nuht." His spelling is a very close phonetic match for Tân Sơn Nhứt / Tan Son Nhut, which hosted extensive U.S. Army aviation activity in 1964-1965 — but the identification remains a hypothesis until unit records place his company headquarters there. Note from the unit research (research/units/callsigns-and-units.md): the 118th Aviation Company was based at Bien Hoa, so if his HQ was truly at Tân Sơn Nhứt, a Saigon-based unit (145th Avn Bn HQ elements, or the UTT/68th at Tan Son Nhut) deserves equal weight. No coordinates are recorded until the identification is settled. 2026-08-23 (giscus-stories-c18118912): asked what base his hooch T 123 stood on, Larry answered with the base name again. The form that reached the page, “Thon Sohn Nuht”, is his son’s transcription and not a further rendering from Larry — the comment itself says “I might be misspelling this.” The identification stays plausible on one witness and no documentary support, and no coordinates are set. 2026-09-05: the base gained a layout in his telling, all of it transcribed from the photographs. The EM Club of the floor-show frames (VN-0030 to VN-0034) was 'about 50 yards from my hooch'; the barracks with his bed and mosquito net is where the gibbon Sarge was photographed (VN-0152); the housekeeper of VN-0151 is placed here; a road to the airport end of the base (VN-0067) is the one 'we had to walk down to get a Taxi,' with 'a machine gun nest at the end of the road,' and an orphanage stood 'at the end of the road… somewhat near the machine gun nest' (VN-0070); the road into Saigon ran past a river where children swam (VN-0064); and the 2½-ton truck he drove for the unit may be the one in VN-0080. Each is his placement of a single frame and is rated as such on the photograph. He also puts the AR-15 of VN-0019 here as 'one of the first ones in Vietnam,' which fits the 1964 issue of the rifle to Army aviation and advisory units before the M16 became general. 2026-09-12: more of the base in his telling, given at the photographs the same day, and a fourth drawing. His company area had "maybe 10 hooches and a big BBQ'ing area with steaks. 24 hours a day you could get a steak" (VN-0026). Everyone in his hooch was "pretty much E-4's", and he slept immediately on the right as you enter the door (VN-0003). The flight line was "probably 3 blocks away" (VN-0012), and he drew it that afternoon, unprompted, with a runway, a helicopter his son reports he drew first to show where Rosethorn 007 stood, two aeroplanes, and boxes for buildings, among them one labelled HANGER and one labelled MOVIE’S, under a heading that begins TON SON NHUT in his hand (VD-0004). The radio van was crewed by about four men by day and one at night, and he often took the night because he was going to the mountain so often: "I felt safer on the mountain that I did at Ton Son Nhut" (VN-0014). "I think the street in the background is 18th Aviation unit" (VN-0011) repeats, three weeks later and at a different photograph, his 2026-08-23 statement that the ops van stood "over in the property of 18th aviation". His son confirms he said 18th, not 118th; the two are separate leads under ledger #2. Another unit "had just arrived maybe a week or so" before VN-0017, which he dates to about July 1965, and the man who did the bathroom shooting was from it (ledger #11). None of this moves the identification, which still rests on the name.
